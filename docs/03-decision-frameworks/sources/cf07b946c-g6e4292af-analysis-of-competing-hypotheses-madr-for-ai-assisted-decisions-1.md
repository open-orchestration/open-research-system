#### What is an ACH?
Analysis of Competing Hypotheses (ACH) is a tool designed to help decision-makers objectively evaluate multiple competing hypotheses (explanations) aimed typically at _**disconfirming**_ _rather than_ _confirming_ a possible explanation or theory (hypothesis)[1](https://zatonski.substack.com/p/analysis-of-competing-hypotheses#footnote-1).
Sounds very complex, but it really isn’t. 
Doctors are trained to use a simplified variant of the ACH called [differential diagnosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_diagnosis) (DDx) - a process of _eliminating_ possible causes causes of patient’s symptoms, and strategically identifying missing information that will allow to narrow down the underlying problem. 
[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!QbjM!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F3c31ac6d-f545-4b15-ac2c-06acb675bd68_1430x598.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!QbjM!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F3c31ac6d-f545-4b15-ac2c-06acb675bd68_1430x598.png)A summary of key differences between ACH and DDx.
Doctors practice the application of ACH (DDx) process multiple times each day, which makes many physicians intuitively proficient with adopting this process outside of clinical practice. 
In fact, the ability to perform ACH is one of the most desirable, and highly transferable skills, explaining why many physicians excel in diverse roles outside of clinical practice. 
This particular skill makes physicians highly valuable in fields beyond medicine, such as intelligence analysis, business strategy, financial forecasting, or policy-making. 
#### Practical value of ACH
Applying the principles of ACH allows you to overcome three very common mistakes that often lead to poor decisions or inaccurate forecasts:
  * People are generally _susceptible_ and unduly influenced by first impressions, previous [assumptions](https://zatonski.substack.com/p/assumptions-kill-assessments-save), existing [biases](https://zatonski.substack.com/p/10-thinking-distortions-that-sabotage), simple and seemingly coherent explanations, previous experiences and prejudices.
  * People rarely _intentionally_ create a full set of possible explanations and outcomes BEFORE the start of any project.
  * People tend to trust the information and evidence that supports their pre-existing assumptions or preferred outcomes, without realising that the same information (or the same evidence) can also be consistent with other explanations.

##### EXERCISE 1
_A Marketing Campaign Evaluation_
A marketing manager, Lucy, is evaluating the success of a recent social media campaign aimed at increasing brand awareness and driving sales for a new product. Lucy strongly believes that social media marketing is the most effective strategy for their target audience. She has recently completed internal training on digital marketing. Lucy has collected the following evidence to guide her conclusions:
  * After the launch, website traffic increased by 25%.
  * Sales for the new product increased by 13% during the campaign period.
  * The campaign received a high number of likes, shares, and positive comments on social media platforms.


Based on the information above, Lucy concludes that:
  * The increased website traffic, higher sales, and positive social media engagement are the result of her campaign.
  * The social media strategy adopted by her team is highly effective


Lucy therefore proposes increasing the budget for future social media campaigns, which her manager, Rebecca, immediately approves.  
  
Now, take a clean piece of paper, and something to write:
  1. Write down at least 5 other possible explanations which equally explain observed increase in website traffic, sales, and social engagement. 
  2. Notice that Lucy has chosen only one of the possible explanations, and attributed the change in metrics to her actions (her campaign). Write down what behaviour and what cognitive fallacy lies behind her decision? How could it be mitigated?
  3. Looking at the list of explanations you have written above, what are the consequences for the business that directly result from Lucy’s proposal and from her manager’s approval?
  4. What could Lucy’s manager do to prevent members of her team from drawing false conclusions? What could Rebecca do to make a more accurate assessment before signing off on the new budget?

#### The Method
In contrast to the [Key Assumptions Check](https://zatonski.substack.com/p/key-assumptions-check-kac) and [Quality of Information Check](https://zatonski.substack.com/p/quality-of-information-check-qic), the Analysis of Competing Hypotheses is a little bit more complex. It is often better performed in a group, and will require writing things down.
In complex clinical scenarios, doctors convene an MDT (Multi-Disciplinary Team meeting) to include multiple experts in deciding the best way forward with patient’s treatment. Outside of clinical practice, a properly conducted ACH requires a small team for optimal results (although certain easy cases, like in the exercise above can be readily performed by an individual).
ACH demands a systematic and explicit identification of all reasonable alternative explanations for observed facts, followed by assigning specific pieces of collected evidence (insights, data) against each hypothesis, rather than evaluating plausibility of each hypothesis one at a time. 
In order to create a “level playing field” for competing explanations, the process must:
  * Ensure that all available information and all available arguments are evaluated. 
  * Nothing must be dismissed, no mater how trivial or irrelevant it might at first appear. 
  * Each piece of information must be treated equally (regardless of how the team _feels_ about it). 
  * Same weight must be given to each information when considering each hypothesis. 
  * Protect the team against tendencies to ignore, discount or dismiss information that does not seem to “fit” comfortably with the preferred explanation.
  * Prevent the team from closing or dismissing a particular explanation or hypothesis prematurely (before the entire process is completed).
