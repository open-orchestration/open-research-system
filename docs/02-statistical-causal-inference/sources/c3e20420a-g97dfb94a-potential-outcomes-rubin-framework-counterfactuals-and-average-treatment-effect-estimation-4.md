## Abstract
Causal inference involves determining whether a treatment (e.g., an education program) causes a change in outcomes (e.g., academic achievement). It is well-known that causal effects are more challenging to estimate than associations. Over the past 50 years, the potential outcomes framework has become one of the most widely used approaches for defining, identifying, and estimating causal effects. In this paper, we review the potential outcomes framework with a focus on potential outcomes notation to define individual and average causal effects. We then show how three canonical assumptions, Unconfoundedness, Positivity, and Consistency, may be used to identify average causal effects. The identification results motivate methods for estimating causal effects in practice, which include model-based estimators, such as regression, inverse probability weighting, and doubly robust estimation, and procedures that target covariate balance, such as matching and stratification. Examples and discussion are grounded in the context of a running example of a study aimed at assessing the causal effect of receipt of special education services on 5th grade mathematics achievement in school-aged children. Practical considerations for education research are discussed.

## Notes
  1. An _estimand_ is the target of inference in a statistical analysis. This is in contrast with an _estimator_ , which gives a mathematical rule for calculating an approximation of the estimand based on data.
  2. As we note in the “[Identifying assumptions for the ATE: characterizing treatment assignment](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12564-024-09957-2#Sec7)” section, this definition of exposure very likely does not satisfy the Consistency assumption, which is one of the canonical assumptions required for causal identification. In that section we discuss how the potential outcome notation might be extended to accommodate Consistency violations.
  3. Although our focus is on causal inference for binary treatment, it is possible to use the POF to define, identify, and estimate causal effects for multi-valued categorical treatment variables (Cattaneo, [2010](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12564-024-09957-2#ref-CR17 "Cattaneo, M. Journal of Econometrics, 155\(2\), 138–154."); McCaffrey et al., [2013](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12564-024-09957-2#ref-CR82 "McCaffrey, D. F., Griffin, B. A., Almirall, D., Slaughter, M. E., Ramchand, R., & Burgette, L. F. \(2013\). A tutorial on propensity score estimation for multiple treatments using generalized boosted models. Statistics in Medicine, 32, 3388–3414.")) and continuous treatment variables (Hirano & Imbens, [2004](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12564-024-09957-2#ref-CR52 "Hirano, K., & Imbens, G. W. \(2004\). The propensity score with continuous treatments. In A. Gelman & X.-L. Meng \(Eds.\), Applied bayesian modeling and causal inference from in complete-data perspectives \(pp. 73–84\). Springer."); Fong et al., [2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12564-024-09957-2#ref-CR39 "Fong, C., Hazlett, C., & Imai, K. \(2018\). Covariate balancing propensity score for a continuous treatment: Application to the efficacy of political advertisements. The Annals of Applied Statistics, 12, 156–177.")).
  4. Individual treatment effects can be defined as any function of the potential outcomes (e.g., log-ratios of \\(Y_i^1\\) and \\(Y_i^0\\)), but we focus on the difference because it is by far the most commonly used definition.
  5. The POF often considers the potential outcomes fixed, such that the only random variable is the treatment assignment \\(Z_i\\). When working with a finite population of subjects, sums may be used in place of expectations; for example, the ATE would be defined as \\(\frac{1}{n} \sum _{i=1}^n \left( Y_i^1 - Y_i^0\right)\\). For discussion of how these finite-population and super-population frameworks compare, see Imbens and Rubin ([2015](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12564-024-09957-2#ref-CR64 "Imbens, G. W., & Rubin, D. B. \(2015\). Causal inference in statistics, social, and biomedical sciences. Cambridge University Press."), Chapter 6) and Ding et al. ([2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12564-024-09957-2#ref-CR35 "Ding, P., Li, X., & Miratrix, L. W. \(2017\). Bridging finite and super population causal inference. Journal of Causal Inference. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1515/jci-2016-0027
                  
                ")). In the remainder, we will assume expectations are taken with respect to an infinite target population.
  6. In the case of linear regression with interactions, one could ensure that the estimated regression coefficient for the treatment \\(Z_i\\) corresponds to \\({\hat{\tau }}_{reg}\\) by including interactions between the treatment and a centered version of the covariates, instead of the covariates themselves. For a discussion on this point, see Lin ([2013](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12564-024-09957-2#ref-CR78 "Lin, W. \(2013\). Agnostic notes on regression adjustments to experimental data: Reexamining freedman’s critique. The Annals of Applied Statistics. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1214/12-AOAS583
                  
                ")).
  7. For a visual representation of the impact of linear adjustments under covariate balance and imbalance, see Keller and Marchev ([2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12564-024-09957-2#ref-CR70 "Keller, B., & Marchev, D. \(2023\). Analysis of covariance: Univariate and multivariate approaches. In R. J. Tierney, F. Rizvi, & K. Erkican \(Eds.\), International Encyclopedia of Education \(pp. 536–547\). Elsevier.")).
  8. In practice, it is almost always a good idea to estimate the propensity score when estimating a causal effect, even if the estimation approach does not use the propensity score, because the estimated propensity score can be used to assess the tenability of the Positivity assumption.

## Appendix: Identification results
### Appendix: Identification results
For the first result, in Eq. [6](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12564-024-09957-2#Equ6),
$$\begin{aligned}&E \left\\{ E(Y_i^\text {obs} | X_i, Z_i = 1) - E(Y_i^\text {obs} | X_i, Z_i = 0) \right\\}&\text {Positivity} \\\&\quad = E \left\\{ E(Y_i^1 | X_i, Z_i = 1) - E(Y_i^0 | X_i, Z_i = 0) \right\\}&\text {Consistency} \\\&\quad = E \left\\{ E(Y_i^1 | X_i) - E(Y_i^0 | X_i) \right\\}&\text {unconfoundedness} \\\&\quad = E \left\\{ E(Y_i^1 - Y_i^0 | X_i) \right\\}&\text {linearity of expectation} \\\&\quad = E(Y_i^1 - Y_i^0).&\text {iterated expectation} \\\ \end{aligned}$$
For the second result, in Eq. [7](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12564-024-09957-2#Equ7), Positivity is required to guarantee the expression is well-defined.
$$\begin{aligned}&E \left\\{ \frac{Y_i^\text {obs} Z_i}{e(X_i)} - \frac{Y_i^\text {obs}(1 - Z_i)}{1 - e(X_i)} \right\\}&\text {Positivity} \\\&\quad = E \left( \frac{Y_i^1 Z_i}{e(X_i)} - \frac{Y_i^0(1 - Z_i)}{1 - e(X_i)} \right)&\text {Consistency} \\\&\quad = E \left\\{ E \left( \frac{Y_i^1 Z_i}{e(X_i)} - \frac{Y_i^0(1 - Z_i)}{1 - e(X_i)} \right) | X_i \right\\}&\text {iterated expectation} \\\&\quad = E \left\\{ E \left( \frac{Y_i^1 Z_i}{e(X_i)} | X_i \right) - E \left( \frac{Y_i^0 (1 - Z_i)}{1 - e(X_i)} | X_i \right) \right\\}&\text {linearity of expectation} \\\&\quad = E \left\\{ \frac{E \left( Y_i^1 Z_i | X_i \right) }{e(X_i)} - \frac{E \left( Y_i^0 (1 - Z_i) | X_i \right) }{1 - e(X_i)} \right\\}&\hbox {} e(X_i) \text { is fixed given} X_i \\\&\quad = E \left\\{ \frac{E \left( Z_i | X_i \right) E \left( Y_i^1 | X_i \right) }{e(X_i)} - \frac{E \left( 1 - Z_i | X_i \right) E \left( Y_i^0 | X_i \right) }{1 - e(X_i)} \right\\}&\text {unconfoundedness} \\\&\quad = E \left\\{ E \left( Y_i^1 | X_i \right) - E \left( Y_i^0 | X_i \right) \right\\}&E \left( Z_i | X_i \right) = e(X_i) \\\&\quad = E \left\\{ E(Y_i^1 - Y_i^0 | X_i) \right\\}&\text { linearity of expectation } \\\&\quad = E(Y_i^1 - Y_i^0).&\text {iterated expectation} \\\ \end{aligned}$$
